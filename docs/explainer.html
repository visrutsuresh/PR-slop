PR-slop

Somebody has to read all this rubbish.

A tool that sorts a mountain of code submissions into three piles, so a volunteer can stop reading the pile that does not matter.

1 · The problem

Free software is maintained by volunteers. They are drowning.

Real numbers. The curl project says it is being flooded to the point of breaking. About one in five of its submissions last year were machine-written filler. One project received a single machine-written submission thirteen thousand lines long.

Every one of those costs the volunteer the same first read as a real one.

2 · Why it exists

Nobody is being malicious. They are chasing a line on a CV.

Two real job adverts, collected during my own internship search:

“Contribution to open-source AI projects”

“Public portfolio, hackathon wins, open-source contributions”

I am not a volunteer maintainer. I am the person standing under that pressure. That is why I picked this problem.

3 · What I built

It sorts the pile into three. That is the whole idea.

Pile 1

Act now.
Good work, and it fixes something people already complained about.

Pile 2

Worth a look.
Real work. Needs a human to read it.

Pile 3

Not merged.
Probably never becoming part of the project.

It never touches anything. It writes a note. A human decides. It cannot accept, reject, close or comment on anyone's work.

4 · How it works

Four workers, and they can go back for more.

The bit that makes it an agent, not a form.

The investigator chooses what to search for. From a title containing none of those words it wrote snippet tab stop limit 10 nested placeholders, using the words a person reporting a problem would use rather than a developer fixing one. It found the right complaint. It chose to search a second time 6 times out of 15.

Why not just one question?

The project has 403 recorded complaints. Far too many to hand over at once. Answering “does this fix something already reported?” means going and looking, then judging what came back.

5 · How I tested it fairly

The answers already existed. I did not have to invent them.

I took 15 real submissions that were already finished and decided. Then I hid the outcome and asked the tool to guess.

What the volunteer actually didCorrect pile
Accepted it, and it fixed a known complaint1
Accepted it, no known complaint2
Did not accept it3

Nobody grades these by hand. The answer is simply what really happened.

Five of each, so guessing gets you 33% and no better.

6 · The trap I nearly fell into

It could open the answer sheet.

I asked it for the answer to one case. It opened the file and told me. Every score after that would have been worthless.

Fix: it now runs in an empty room with the door locked. No file reading, no searching, no internet. A test has to prove it is blind before anything else runs.

The two 73.3% figures are not the same thing. The middle one gets there by being generous to everything, so it finds one in five of the pile you wanted to skip. The agent finds four in five. Same headline, four times better at the job.

8 · What I am not hiding

Three things that make us look worse.

Step 2 did nothing.

The checking step caught zero mistakes across all 15. Once step 1 gave it real complaints to point at, it stopped making them up. Step 1 earned the result. I kept step 2 because it is free, not because it proved itself.

I measured the wrong thing at first.

My first score said the simple version cited real evidence 86.7% of the time, which appeared to disprove my own prediction. It did not. Twelve of its thirteen “correct” citations were file names copied straight out of the question. That is free. Counting only things it had to go and find, it scored 1 of 3, and my prediction held.

It is bad at the pile that matters most.

It spots accepted work perfectly, 5 of 5 twice over. It spots rejected work 1 of 5. That is the wrong way round for a tool whose job is finding the pile you can safely ignore.

9 · What I learned

“Rejected” almost never means “bad”.

Only one of seventeen was the thing everyone complains about. The rest were robots doing chores, or the team quietly replacing their own work.

So the honest claim is smaller than the exciting one. A tool built on what a project records for free measures what people did, not whether the work was any good. That is genuinely useful. It is not the same as “this detects rubbish”, and I would rather say so than let the big number imply it.

10 · Check it yourself

Three commands. No account, no payment, no internet.

./run.sh eval

./run.sh baseline

./run.sh advanced

Every answer the tool ever gave is saved in the project. Those commands re-add the scores from the saved answers, so you get exactly the numbers above, every time, on any machine.

Why not just re-run it live? Because these tools are not repeatable. Ask twice, get different words. Saved answers are the only thing that gives everyone the same result.
