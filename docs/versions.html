PR-slop

Six attempts. One number. Here is what it all means.

What each version changed, how the score is worked out, and why the table looks the way it does.

Part 1 · How the score works

Sort 15 things into 3 piles. Count how many landed right.

We have 15 real submissions whose fate is already known. Five belong in each pile. The tool guesses; we compare.

Why not just count correct answers out of 15?

Because a lazy tool could put everything in the biggest pile and look decent. So we score each pile separately, then average. A tool has to do well at all three.

Worked example, the version we shipped

1Act now — got 3 of 5 right= 0.60
2Worth a look — got 4 of 5 right= 0.80
3Not merged — got 4 of 5 right= 0.80

(0.60 + 0.80 + 0.80) ÷ 3 = 0.733, which we write as 73.3%.

Pure guessing scores 33.3%. Three piles, so one in three by luck. That is the floor. Anything at 33% has added nothing.

And one case is worth 6.7 points. With only 5 items per pile, a single item changing its mind moves the headline by nearly 7. Two versions within 7 points of each other are not meaningfully different. Remember that when you read the table.

Part 2 · The other number

“Good work wrongly rejected” is the one that would get you shouted at.

So we track it on its own. 0 of 10 means: of the 10 submissions that were genuinely accepted in real life, it wrongly rejected none.

Part 3 · The six versions

Each one changed exactly one thing, on purpose.

v1 The first real agent

Four workers instead of one. One writes its own searches and can search again. One reads the real source code and checks whether the submission does what it claims. One decides the pile. One checks the answer and can send it back.

46.7% — far worse than the simple script. It wrongly rejected good work 4 times in 10.

Why: the code-checker said “the code does not match the description” on three submissions, and the decider threw all three in the reject pile. All three had actually been accepted. It confused “I do not rate this code” with “this was not accepted”.

v2 Told it those are different questions

Added: a “no” from the code-checker is about whether the description is accurate. It says nothing about whether anyone accepted the work.

Wrong rejections: 4 → 1. Headline unchanged at 46.7%.

Why no better: it stopped rejecting and started shrugging instead. Three times it answered “cannot tell”, which scores as wrong.

v3 Made it commit, and told it the odds

Two changes. No shrugging allowed — a sorting tool that refuses to sort leaves you reading everything anyway. And it was told a true fact it never had: about 9 in 10 submissions here were accepted. Rejection is the rare case.

46.7% → 73.3%

It had been treating all three piles as equally likely, so every flicker of doubt pushed work into reject.

v4 Fixed the workers talking to each other  ← shipped

Two faults found by reading the actual logs. The searcher's conclusion arrived at the decider as a bare number with no explanation, so the decider ignored it. And the searcher was throwing away correct matches for not being worded closely enough.

73.3%, and wrong rejections back to 0 of 10, and the reject pile up to 0.80.

Worth noticing: both faults were handover problems. Every worker behaved sensibly on its own. The system still got it wrong because information was lost between them.

v5 Overcorrected, badly

Told the decider it must accept the searcher's conclusion.

73.3% → 46.7%

My mistake. In v4 I had loosened the searcher so it accepts near-matches. Then in v5 I made it the final word. Loose plus final word means nearly everything gets called “act now”. Pile 1 went perfect, the other two collapsed.

v6 Let it say how sure it was

The searcher now rates itself: certain, likely, or just a guess. Only “certain” carries weight.

46.7% → 66.7%, still below v4.

Each pile column is “how many of those 5 did it get right”. 0.80 means 4 out of 5.

Look along the two rows that both say 73.3%.

The simple script gets 1.00 and 1.00 on the two “accepted” piles, and 0.20 on reject. It wins by being generous to everything. It finds one in five of the pile you wanted to skip.

v4 finds four in five of that pile, while still never binning good work. Same headline. Four times better at the actual job.

Why I stopped at six. One case is worth 6.7 points, and I have now tried six times against the same 15 answers. That is how you talk yourself into a number that falls apart on the sixteenth case. Carrying on would have produced a better-looking figure and a worse tool.

Part 5 · In one line

Nothing beat the simple script on the headline. Something beat it at the job.

Six honest attempts, two of them my own overcorrections, all kept in the record with their numbers. The one we ship ties on the score everyone looks at and is four times better at finding the pile a maintainer actually wants to stop reading.
